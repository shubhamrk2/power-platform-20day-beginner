The Big Question for September — What IS Microsoft 365 and why does it exist?

Imagine you are starting a new company. You need: email for all employees, file storage everyone can share, a tool for video calls and meetings, a way to track customers, and security so only your employees can see company files.

Before Microsoft 365 existed, you bought all these separately — an email server (expensive hardware), a file server, a video call license, a security system — and paid a tech expert to keep it all running. If a hard drive broke, your emails were gone.

Microsoft 365 says: "Give us a monthly fee per person, and we run ALL of this for you in our data centers around the world. No hardware. No expert. No single point of failure."

This is called Software as a Service (SaaS) — you pay for software as if it were water or electricity. You do not own the pipe, you just pay for what flows through it.

💭 System Thinking — What are the risks of giving ALL your data to Microsoft?

Think about this genuinely before reading the answer.

  • If Microsoft has an outage (Azure went down in 2021 — thousands of companies could not work for hours), your entire company stops
  • If Microsoft raises prices, you must pay or lose everything
  • If governments force Microsoft to share data, your company's private information may be exposed
  • If you stop paying, Microsoft locks your data — you must export before that happens

This is why companies use a "multi-cloud" or "hybrid" strategy — not everything in one place. You will learn about this in November.

The Cloud, Tenants, Identity & the Microsoft Ecosystem

Vocabulary for Week 1 — Learn these before doing anything. Read each one out loud.
1. Cloud (noun)
Computers that belong to someone else (Microsoft, Amazon, Google) that you rent over the internet.
Analogy: Electricity. You do not own a power station. You pay your bill and power comes from the grid.
Example: "We moved our company email to the cloud so we do not need to maintain our own email server."
2. Tenant (noun)
Your company's private space inside Microsoft's cloud.
Analogy: An apartment building. Microsoft owns the building. Your company rents one apartment. Your neighbour company has their own apartment — they cannot enter yours.
Example: "Our company's tenant is on Microsoft's India data center. All our users exist only inside our tenant."
3. License (noun)
Permission to use a software feature, given to one person.
Analogy: A bus ticket. The bus exists for everyone, but you can only ride if you have a ticket. If your ticket expires, you cannot ride.
Example: "Pooja has a Microsoft 365 E5 license, so she can use all premium features. Rohit has only a basic license."
4. Identity (noun, in technology)
Proof of who you are in a computer system — your username, password, and what you are allowed to do.
Analogy: Your Aadhaar card. It proves who you are, what benefits you are entitled to, and where you can go.
Example: "When Sanjay signs in, the system checks his identity. Because his identity has 'Library Staff' role, he can see the book inventory."
5. Azure Active Directory / Entra ID (noun)
Microsoft's system for managing identities — who can sign in, what they can see, and what they can do.
Analogy: The reception desk and security system of an office building. If your badge says "Finance Department," you can only go to the Finance floor.
Example: "Entra ID knows Neha is a Junior Librarian. When she tries to delete a book record, Entra ID blocks her — Junior Librarians only have Read access."
6. Role (noun)
A set of permissions grouped under one name.
Analogy: A job title. "Manager" can approve expenses. "Employee" can submit expenses but not approve. The title defines what you can do.
Example: "The System Administrator role gives full control. We gave Rohit the Basic User role because he is a library member, not staff."
7. Provision (verb)
To set up or create a resource or system, making it ready to use.
Analogy: When you rent an apartment, the landlord provisions it — connects electricity, water, gas before you move in.
Example: "It takes 2–3 minutes to provision a new Power Platform environment because Microsoft is setting up the database in the background."
8. Domain (noun)
The name of your company's online address — the part after the @ in your email.
Analogy: Your home address. If your address is "123, Library Lane, Delhi," your domain is "LibraryLane.in."
Example: "Our trial tenant uses the domain accenturetrial.onmicrosoft.com. All user emails end with @accenturetrial.onmicrosoft.com."

💭 System Thinking — Why three separate environments (DEV, UAT, PRD)?

What would happen if there was only one environment?

Imagine you are changing how loan records are created. While you are building and testing, your half-finished broken feature is live for all members. Rohit opens the app to borrow a book and sees errors. He cannot borrow. The library stops working.

This is called "developing in production" — one of the most dangerous mistakes in software.

  • DEV = Your workshop. Messy, experimental, always changing.
  • UAT (User Acceptance Testing) = A clean copy where real users test before launch. If Rohit finds a bug here, nothing is actually broken for real.
  • PRD (Production) = The real thing. Members use it. It must always work.

This pattern — DEV → UAT → PRD — is used by every company in the world, from a 5-person startup to Google.

Concept — Microsoft Entra ID: The Identity System

Entra ID (previously called Azure Active Directory) is the most important piece of the entire Microsoft ecosystem. It is the brain that decides who can do what.

How sign-in works — step by step (when Sanjay signs into Power Apps):

Step 1: Sanjay types username + password at make.powerapps.com
         ↓
Step 2: Power Apps sends credentials to Entra ID:
        "Hey Entra ID, is this person real?"
         ↓
Step 3: Entra ID checks its database:
        - Is sanjay.singh@[tenant] a real user? ✅
        - Is his password correct? ✅
        - Does he have an active license? ✅
        - Is MFA required? → If yes, Sanjay gets a code on his phone
         ↓
Step 4: Entra ID issues a TOKEN — a temporary digital pass
        (Like a wristband at a concert — proves you paid without
        showing your ticket every 5 seconds)
         ↓
Step 5: Power Apps reads the token, sees Sanjay's roles:
        "Library Staff role + Basic User role"
        Shows Sanjay only what his roles allow

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):

The E5 license includes Azure AD Premium P2, which lets you enforce MFA. Why it matters: Neha uses password "Library2024" everywhere. A hacker steals it from a different website and tries it on your tenant — it works. But with MFA, they also need Neha's phone. When they try to sign in, Neha's phone gets a notification: "Someone is trying to sign in. Approve?" She taps Deny. The hacker is blocked.

💭 System Thinking — Why use Tokens instead of sending username/password every click?

Without tokens: Every time Sanjay clicks something, the app sends his password to Microsoft. This is very slow (network call every click) and very dangerous (password travels the network constantly — hackers love this).

With tokens: The token is issued ONCE when you log in. It says "Sanjay is authenticated, Library Staff role, valid for 1 hour." The app trusts the token. After 1 hour, the token is silently refreshed.

Analogy: A hotel key card. You check in once (authenticate). The hotel gives you a key card (token) that opens your room for your stay. The front desk is not following you to check your ID every time you open your room door.

Most people never look at logs — until something goes wrong. Smart administrators look at them regularly. After September 30, you will no longer have access to these premium sign-in logs. That is another reason to use them now.

🚫 Common English Mistakes — Week 1

Mistake 1: Missing articles (the / a / an)
❌ "I created user in tenant."
✅ "I created a user in the tenant."
Rule: Use "a/an" the first time you mention something. Use "the" when both of you know which specific thing you mean.

Mistake 2: "I am having" instead of "I have"
❌ "I am having a question about licenses."
✅ "I have a question about licenses."
Rule: Verbs like have, know, understand, need, want, belong — do NOT use the -ing form.

Mistake 3: "informations" / "feedbacks" / "knowledges"
❌ "I found many informations in the admin center."
✅ "I found a lot of information in the admin center."
Rule: These words have NO plural: information, knowledge, feedback, data, advice, equipment, access. Never add -s.

Mistake 4: "How I can..." instead of "How can I..."
❌ "How I can add a new user?"
✅ "How can I add a new user?"
Rule: In questions, the helping verb (can, should, will, does) comes BEFORE the subject (I, you, we).

Vocabulary for Week 2
9. SharePoint (noun)
Microsoft's system for storing and sharing files and lists inside a company, accessible from anywhere.
Analogy: The office notice board plus the filing cabinet — digital and accessible from anywhere in the world.
Example: "We store the library's event calendar in a SharePoint list. Every librarian can see it from their laptop or phone, and changes are updated instantly."
10. List (noun, in SharePoint)
A table of data stored in SharePoint — like an Excel spreadsheet, but with sharing, permissions, and workflows built in.
Analogy: A physical ledger. The "Book Loan Register" is a list — each row is one loan, each column is one piece of information.
Example: "I created a SharePoint list called 'Library Events' with columns for Event Name, Date, Location, and Maximum Capacity."
11. Library (noun, in SharePoint)
A special type of SharePoint storage for files (documents, images, videos) — NOT the same as a book library!
Analogy: A folder on your computer, but shared with the whole team and with full version history.
Example: "The library policy documents are stored in a Document Library. When the Librarian edits the Borrowing Policy, SharePoint keeps the old version too."
12. Version History (noun)
A record of every change made to a file or document, who made it, and when.
Analogy: The "undo" button, but for weeks or months. You can see what a document looked like 3 weeks ago and restore it.
Example: "Someone deleted the 'Late Fees' section from the borrowing policy. I went to version history and restored the document from 2 days ago in 30 seconds."
13. Permission (noun)
What a specific person or group is allowed to do — read, edit, delete, or share.
Analogy: Different keys for different rooms. The librarian has a key to the storage room. A member has a key to the reading room only.
Example: "Rohit has Read permission on the events list, so he can see events but cannot add or delete them. Pooja has Edit permission."
14. Teams Channel (noun)
A discussion room inside Microsoft Teams for a specific topic or team.
Analogy: WhatsApp groups, but inside a company tool, with files, meetings, and apps attached.
Example: "We created a 'Book Acquisitions' channel in Teams so librarians can discuss which new books to buy. All conversation is recorded and searchable."
15. OneDrive (noun)
Your personal cloud storage in Microsoft 365 — only you can see it unless you share.
Analogy: Your personal locker. Your team has a shared office (SharePoint), but your locker (OneDrive) is just for you.
Example: "I keep work-in-progress reports in OneDrive. When they are finished and ready for the team, I move them to the SharePoint document library."

💭 System Thinking — If SharePoint lists are free, why don't all companies use them for everything?

The answer: Complexity and scale.

A library with 50 books and 30 members can use SharePoint lists. But a library with 5,000 books, 10,000 members, and 500 loans per day? SharePoint lists slow down, become hard to manage, and cannot handle complex data relationships (a loan connects to a book, which connects to an author, which connects to a publisher — 4 levels of relationship, which SharePoint cannot handle cleanly).

Dataverse is a proper relational database. SharePoint lists are a useful collaboration tool that happens to store data. Knowing which one to use for which situation is a skill that senior architects have. You are learning it now.

Exercise 3 — Build the Library Events List in SharePoint

Step 1: Open SharePoint

  • Go to microsoft365.com → sign in → click the SharePoint app
  • You will see the SharePoint home — a list of sites you have access to

Step 2: Create a Team Site for the Library

  • Click Create

  • Click the View dropdown (top right, near "All Items") → Create new view
  • Name: Upcoming Events — Sort by: Event_Date Ascending

  • Click OK — now the list shows only future events, sorted by date automatically for everyone
💭 System Thinking — Why create VIEWS instead of sorting manually every time?

Without views: Pooja sorts the list by date. Sanjay opens the same list and sees it unsorted (because his browser has its own state). Every person has to sort manually. If 10 people use the list, each person wastes time sorting every time.

With views: The "Upcoming Events" view is saved once. Everyone who clicks it sees the same sorted, filtered list automatically. Changes to the data update automatically.

This concept — saving a reusable configuration — appears everywhere in technology: database views, Power BI report filters, Power Apps gallery filters, GitHub branch protections. The principle is always the same: define it once, use it everywhere.

Concept — Microsoft Teams: More Than Chat

Most people think Teams is just a video call and chat tool. In the Microsoft ecosystem, Teams is a platform — it integrates with almost everything:

  1. Teams + SharePoint: Every Teams channel has a Files tab that IS a SharePoint document library. When you share a file in Teams, it is stored in SharePoint automatically.
  2. Teams + Power Apps: You can embed a Power App directly inside a Teams tab. Library members could open Teams and see the Library App inside it.
  3. Teams + Power Automate: You can build flows that send messages to Teams channels ("New book 'Atomic Habits' added to the library") or ask for approval in Teams ("Priyanka requests membership upgrade — Approve or Reject").
  4. Teams + Copilot Studio: You can publish your library chatbot directly into Teams. Library members type questions in Teams and the chatbot answers.
Add the Library Events List as a Teams Tab
  • Open Microsoft Teams at teams.microsoft.com
  • Create a team called "Community Library Staff" if it does not exist
  • Go to the "General" channel → click the + button (Add a tab)
  • Search for SharePoint → click it → select the "Community Library" site and the "Library Events" list
  • Click Save — now the list appears inside Teams. Librarians never need to navigate to SharePoint separately.
💭 System Thinking — Why does Microsoft integrate all its products so tightly?

Business strategy: If Teams integrates with SharePoint, Outlook, Power Apps, and Dataverse — and you use all of them — you are deeply embedded in the Microsoft ecosystem. Switching to a competitor (Google Workspace, Slack, Salesforce) means losing all these integrations. This is called a "switching cost" — the pain of leaving one platform for another.

This is not evil — it is a business model. All big platforms do this (Apple, Google, Amazon). Understanding this helps you make smarter decisions about which tools to choose and which ones to depend on too heavily.

Concept — How Power Platform Fits Together: One Data Source, Four Lenses 
POWER APPS          POWER AUTOMATE
(user interface)    (automation)
       ↕                  ↕
         DATAVERSE (the data)
       ↕                  ↕
POWER BI            COPILOT STUDIO
(insight)           (chatbot AI)

All four tools read and write to the same Dataverse. This is the entire design principle of Power Platform — one data source, four different lenses. In our library:

  • Power Apps (Canvas): Rohit opens the app, searches "Atomic Habits," borrows it → creates a Loan row in Dataverse
  • Power Automate: The new Loan row triggers a flow → sends Rohit a confirmation email → schedules a due-date reminder

All four tools. One data source. One community library.

💭 System Thinking — What happens to all four tools if Dataverse goes down?

All four break simultaneously. The app cannot save loans, flows cannot read data, Power BI shows errors, the chatbot cannot answer questions.

This is the risk of a single point of failure. Advanced architects add redundancy — backups, failover databases, offline caching. We will not build this level of complexity in our project, but understanding that it IS a risk is the first step to designing safe systems.

💭 System Thinking — If a book gets borrowed, who or what should update "Available_Copies"?

Option A: The librarian manually changes Available_Copies when a loan is created → Bad. Humans make mistakes. If the librarian forgets, the data is wrong.

Option B: A Power Automate flow automatically decreases Available_Copies when a Loan is created → Better. But what if two loans are created at the exact same time? (This is called a "race condition.") The count could be wrong.

Option C: Available_Copies is never stored — it is always calculated fresh: Total_Copies - COUNT(open loans for this book) → Best for accuracy. Can never be wrong. Downside: it is slower (a calculation, not a stored number).

This is a real architectural debate in data systems. The trade-off: fast reading (store the result) vs always accurate (calculate fresh). In November when we learn SQL, we will build both and compare.

Concept — What Is Copilot Studio and Why Does It Exist?

Before AI existed, if you wanted to help users answer questions without a human, you had two options:

  1. A FAQ page — users read it themselves (they often cannot find the answer)
  2. A decision-tree bot — if user says "X" then say "Y" (brittle, breaks when users phrase things differently)

Both were frustrating. Users still emailed or called. Copilot Studio is a third option: It uses AI (large language models — the same technology behind ChatGPT) to understand what the user is asking — even if they phrase it unexpectedly. Then it either follows a scripted path or generates an answer from your documents.

The two types of answers:

USER'S QUESTION
       ↓
Does a Topic match this question?
       ↓
   YES ──────────────────────────→ SCRIPTED ANSWER
   (trigger phrase matches)         (you designed exactly what the bot says)
       ↓
    NO
       ↓
Is Generative Answers enabled?
       ↓
   YES ──────────────────────────→ AI-GENERATED ANSWER
   (knowledge sources exist)        (AI reads your documents and creates a response)
       ↓
    NO
       ↓
        BOT SAYS: "I'm sorry, I don't know the answer to that."
💭 System Thinking — When to use a scripted Topic vs Generative Answers?

Use scripted Topics when:

  • The answer must be precise and cannot vary ("The library is open 9 AM to 8 PM")
  • The question requires taking an action ("Check if this book is available" — the bot must query Dataverse)
  • Legal or compliance language must be exact

Use Generative Answers when:

  • There are thousands of possible questions you cannot predict
  • The documents already contain the correct answers
  • Approximate, natural-language answers are acceptable

The risk of Generative Answers: AI can "hallucinate" — it can confidently give a wrong answer. This is why you always test your bot carefully and why important decisions (approving a loan, cancelling a membership) should NEVER be handled by generative AI alone — they need a scripted flow with human confirmation.

💭 Key Principle from Copilot Studio — AI vs Database

Why do we use a scripted topic for book availability instead of letting the AI answer from the knowledge documents?

🚫 Common English Mistakes — Week 4

Mistake 1: "I have done" vs "I did" — confusion about past tenses
Use simple past for a specific finished action: ✅ "Yesterday, I exported the solution."
Use present perfect when the result matters now: ✅ "I have exported the solution — it is ready for import."

Mistake 2: Run-on sentences (too many ideas, no punctuation)
❌ "I exported the solution and imported it and then I tested it and it worked but the SharePoint connection needed to be fixed."
✅ "I exported and imported the solution. After testing, I found that the SharePoint connection had expired. I fixed it by re-authenticating in Power Automate."
Rule: One sentence = one idea.

Mistake 3: "Out of station" (Indian English for "not in the office")
❌ "Pooja was out of station this week."
✅ "Pooja was out of the office this week." OR "Pooja was unavailable this week."

Mistake 4: Vague descriptions in technical writing
❌ "Something went wrong with the import."
✅ "The import failed with an authentication error on the SharePoint connector. Error code: AADSTS70011."
Rule: Always say: what failed, where, and what the exact error was.
