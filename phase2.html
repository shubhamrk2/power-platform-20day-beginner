Read First

Power Fx — the formula language you will use every day

~30 minutes · Read this entire section before starting Day 5

Canvas apps are controlled entirely by formulas you type in a thing called the formula bar. These formulas are called Power Fx. You don't need to be a programmer — Power Fx was designed to feel like Excel formulas. If you've ever typed =SUM(A1:A5) in Excel, you already know the idea.

This section teaches you everything you need to read and write the formulas you'll encounter in Days 5–8. Read it once, try the practice at the end, and you'll be ready.

Concept — what the formula bar is and how to use it

When you open a canvas app in the Studio (editor), you will see a bar at the very top of the screen — just above the canvas where you design your app. That is the formula bar.

  • On the left side of the formula bar, there is a property dropdown — a list of every setting a control has. For example, a Label has a "Text" property, a "Color" property, a "Visible" property, and dozens more.
  • On the right side, there is a text box where you type the formula for the selected property.

How to use it, step by step:

  1. Click on any control on the canvas — for example, click a Label.
  2. Look at the property dropdown on the left of the formula bar. It probably says Text.
  3. Look at the formula box on the right. It might say "Label1".
  4. Click inside the formula box. Delete the text and type "Hello World". Press Enter.
  5. The Label now shows "Hello World" on the canvas.

That's it. That's the formula bar. You click a control → choose a property → type a formula. Everything in a canvas app works this way.

Concept — the three rules of Power Fx syntax

Power Fx has three basic rules. Learn these three and you can read any formula.

Rule 1: Text must be in double quotes.
Anything that is plain text must be wrapped in double quotes: "Hello", "Save", "Library Manager". Numbers and formulas do NOT go in quotes. Write 22 not "22" for a font size. Write RGBA(47,111,237,1) not "RGBA(47,111,237,1)" for a colour.

Rule 2: To get a control's value, write ControlName.PropertyName.
The dot ( . ) is how you read a value from something:
SearchInput.Text → "whatever text the user typed in the SearchInput box right now"
BookForm.Mode → "which mode the form is in right now (View, Edit, or New)"
BookGallery.Selected → "which row the user last clicked in the gallery"
App.Width → "the total width of the screen in pixels"

Rule 3: Functions are written with a capital letter and brackets.
If(condition, valueIfTrue, valueIfFalse)
Navigate(ScreenName, ScreenTransition.Fade)
Search(TableName, textToSearch, "columnLogicalName")
You will be given the exact formula to type in each step. You don't need to memorise them — you need to understand what each part means.

Concept — how to read errors (red squiggles)

When a formula has a mistake, Power Fx underlines it in red. Here's how to diagnose and fix errors:

  1. Hover over the red underline — a tooltip shows the error message in plain English. For example: "Name isn't valid. 'BookGallery' isn't recognized."
  2. Check the name of the control. The most common beginner mistake is a typo in a control name. If you named your gallery "BooksList" but the formula says "BookGallery", it won't work. Open the Tree view, check the exact name, and fix the typo.
  3. Check your quotes. Text must be in straight double quotes: "text". If you copied a formula from a document and it has curly quotes like "text", Power Fx will not understand it. Delete and retype the quotes inside the studio.
  4. Check semicolons vs commas. If your computer's regional settings use a semicolon as the list separator (common in India and Europe), Power Fx may want If(condition; yes; no) instead of If(condition, yes, no). Power Apps will usually fix this automatically — if it does, let it.
  5. Warnings (yellow) vs errors (red). A yellow squiggle is a warning — the formula will still work, but Power Fx is flagging something to be aware of. A red squiggle is an error — the formula will not work until fixed.
Concept — RGBA colours explained

In canvas apps, colours are written as RGBA(red, green, blue, alpha) where:

  • Red, Green, Blue — numbers from 0 to 255 for how much of each colour to mix.
  • Alpha — transparency from 0 (fully transparent) to 1 (fully opaque). You'll almost always use 1.

Examples: RGBA(47, 111, 237, 1) → the blue accent colour used in this course. RGBA(255, 255, 255, 1) → pure white. RGBA(0, 0, 0, 1) → pure black. RGBA(220, 53, 69, 1) → red (for delete buttons).

You don't need to memorise these. The colour picker in the Properties pane generates RGBA values for you — just pick a colour visually and the formula appears automatically. But you need to recognise it when you see it.

Day 5

Canvas app foundations: the studio, controls, and the formula bar

~100 minutes

Concept — canvas app vs model-driven app

Power Apps gives you two completely different ways to build an app, and understanding the difference is essential:

  • Canvas app — you design every screen, pixel by pixel, by dragging controls onto a blank canvas. Total creative control. You decide where the title goes, what colour the buttons are, what happens on each tap. Think of it as building a custom mobile app or web form, but without writing front-end code. Better for field workers, customer-facing apps, and tailored experiences.
  • Model-driven app — you point it at your data model (tables, views, forms) and Power Apps auto-generates a professional, fully-featured app. The look is standardised (no pixel control), but you get built-in features: activity timelines, business process flows, command bars, automatic mobile layout, offline capability. Better for back-office data entry and management apps where speed and data depth matter more than custom design.

Both apps read and write the same Dataverse tables. This phase builds one of each over the same data.

Concept — the app studio: every panel explained

When you open a canvas app for editing, you see the Studio. Here is every panel:

  • Tree view (left panel, first icon) — a list of every screen and every control on each screen, in a hierarchy. Click anything here to select it. Always rename controls here — BookGallery not Gallery3.
  • Data panel (left panel, cylinder icon) — where you add and manage data sources (Dataverse tables, SharePoint lists, etc.).
  • Media panel (left panel, image icon) — upload images, audio, or video files used in the app.
  • Canvas (centre) — the visual editing area. Drag controls, resize them, arrange them.
  • Properties pane (right) — when you select a control, its properties appear here: text, colour, font, position, size. You can set values directly in these fields.
  • Formula bar (top, above the canvas) — shows the formula for the selected property of the selected control. Click the property dropdown on the left of the formula bar to switch between properties.
  • Insert menu (top toolbar) — the menu to add new controls: Button, Label, Text input, Gallery, Form, etc.
  • Play ▶ button (top right) — runs a preview of the app in a new window. Press Esc to return to editing.
Do this — create the app and set up screens
  • Go to Solutions → My Learning Solution → New → App → Canvas app. Name = Library Manager. Layout = Tablet. Click Create. The Studio opens.
  • In the Tree view, right-click Screen1 → Rename → type BooksScreen. This will be the main browse screen.
  • Click + New screen (the button at the bottom of the Tree view or from Insert menu) → Blank. Rename it BookFormScreen. Repeat once more → Rename to LoanFormScreen. You now have 3 screens in the tree view.
  • Open the Data panel (cylinder icon). Click Add data. Search for Dataverse. Select it. Search for and add: Books, Authors, Members, Loans. All four tables are now available as data sources throughout the app.
  • Click on BooksScreen in the tree view. Insert a Rectangle (Insert → Rectangle). Set its properties one at a time in the Properties pane on the right:
    Width = App.Width → means "match the width of the whole app screen" — this makes the header span the full width on any device.
    Height = 60 → 60 pixels tall.
    X = 0, Y = 0 → position it at the very top-left corner.
    Fill = RGBA(47, 111, 237, 1) → the blue colour (47 red, 111 green, 237 blue, fully opaque). This is your header bar.
  • Insert a Label. Set Text = "Library Manager", Color = White, Font size = 22, FontWeight = Bold, X = 20, Y = 0, Height = 60. This is the app title in the header.
  • Press Play ▶ to see your header bar. Press Esc to return. Press Ctrl+S to save.
Words you learned today
  • Canvas app — an app you design screen-by-screen by dragging controls; total layout control.
  • Model-driven app — an app auto-generated from your tables; standard look, rich features.
  • Studio — the canvas app editor with Tree view, Canvas, Properties pane, and Formula bar.
  • Screen / control / property — one page / an element on it / a setting on that element.
  • Tree view — the hierarchical list of all screens and controls; always rename controls here.
  • Data source — a Dataverse table (or other service) connected to the app.
Real world — canvas vs model-driven in enterprise

Professional Power Platform architects choose app type based on the audience and use case:

  • Field worker apps are almost always canvas: a construction safety inspector needs a custom checklist app with large touch targets, a camera button, and GPS location capture — model-driven doesn't support that level of custom layout.
  • Back-office admin tools are almost always model-driven: the same company's office staff manage work orders through a model-driven app that auto-generates professional forms, includes activity timelines, and adapts to any screen size without extra work.
  • Many real solutions use both: the field app (canvas) writes to Dataverse; the office app (model-driven) reads and manages the same rows. One database, two apps, two audiences.

Knowing both types — and when to choose each — is a key skill interviewers test for Power Apps roles.

Common mistakes in the app studio
  • Leaving controls named Gallery1, Button2, etc. When you have 15 controls with default names, it's impossible to read a formula like Gallery2.Selected. Rename every control before wiring up formulas — it takes 5 seconds and saves hours of confusion.
  • Working in the wrong screen. The tree view shows all screens. If you accidentally drop a button on BooksScreen when you meant BookFormScreen, nothing will appear where you expect. Always click the target screen in the tree view before inserting controls.
  • Pressing Play without saving first. If Power Apps crashes during Play mode, unsaved changes are lost. Save with Ctrl+S before every Play test.

Explore every property available for a Label: Color, Fill, Font, FontSize, Bold, Visible, OnSelect, X, Y, Width, Height. Don't change anything — just read the list. Day 6

BooksScreen: gallery, search, and Power Fx formulas

~110 minutes

Concept — Power Fx: the formula language of canvas apps

Power Fx is the language you type into the formula bar. It looks exactly like Excel formulas — in fact it was designed to feel familiar to anyone who knows Excel. Key ideas:

  • You refer to a control by its name: SearchInput, BookGallery.
  • You get a control's value by name + dot + property: SearchInput.Text = "whatever is typed in the SearchInput box right now".
  • Some properties are events — they run code when something happens. OnSelect runs when a button is clicked. OnVisible runs when a screen is navigated to.
  • Functions are capitalized and work like Excel: If(, Filter(, Search(, Navigate(.
  • Use straight quotes "like this", not curly quotes "like this" — curly quotes cause errors.
Concept — Gallery and how Items works

A Gallery is a scrollable list. Its Items property controls what rows appear. Set Items = Books and it shows all books. Use the Search() function to filter by what the user types.

Inside the gallery, ThisItem refers to "the row this particular gallery card is displaying." So ThisItem.Title means "the Title of the book on this particular card." This is how galleries show different data on each card automatically.

From outside the gallery, BookGallery.Selected means "whichever row the user last clicked on." This is how you pass the selected book to a form screen.

Do this — build BooksScreen: gallery and search
  • Click BooksScreen in the tree view. Go to Insert → Gallery → Vertical gallery. A gallery appears on the canvas — resize it to fill the screen below your header (set Y = 120 or so).
  • In the Tree view, right-click the gallery → Rename → BookGallery.
  • With BookGallery selected, look at the Properties pane on the right. Set Data source = Books. The gallery fills with your 5 book rows immediately.
  • In the Properties pane, set Layout = "Title, subtitle, and body". Now each card shows three lines. Click inside the gallery (double-click to enter the gallery template). Click the Title1 label. In the formula bar, confirm its Text property = ThisItem.Title.
  • Click Subtitle1 inside the gallery. Set its Text = ThisItem.Genre — this shows the choice label (e.g. "Children").
  • Click Body1 inside the gallery. Set its Text = "Pages: " & Text(ThisItem.Pages) — the & joins text, and Text() converts the number to text for joining.
  • Press Esc to exit the gallery template. Now add a Text input for search: Insert → Input → Text input. Position it above the gallery. Rename it to SearchInput. In the Properties pane, set HintText = "Search by title…".
  • Click BookGallery. Change its Items property in the formula bar to:
    Search(Books, SearchInput.Text, "cr_title")
    Breaking this down: Search( is the function. Books is the table to search through. SearchInput.Text is "whatever the user typed in the SearchInput box" — it updates live as they type. "cr_title" is the logical column name to search in. To find your logical name: go to Dataverse → Book table → Title column → Advanced options → copy the Logical name field. If your prefix is learn, it will be "learn_title". If it starts with cr, use "cr_title". Replace accordingly.
  • Add a Button for adding new books: Insert → Button. Rename to NewBookBtn. Set Text = "+ New Book". Set OnSelect = NewForm(BookForm); Navigate(BookFormScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade).
    Breaking this down: The OnSelect property runs when the button is clicked. The semicolon ( ; ) separates two actions that happen one after the other. NewForm(BookForm) tells the form to go into "New/blank" mode. Navigate(BookFormScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade) takes the user to the BookFormScreen with a fade animation. (BookForm doesn't exist yet — you'll add it on Day 7. Power Fx shows a yellow warning but it's fine.)
  • Select BookGallery. Set its OnSelect property = Navigate(BookFormScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade). Now clicking any book row will navigate to the form screen.
  • Play ▶ — type in the search box and watch the gallery filter in real time. Click a book row — you go to BookFormScreen (blank for now). Press Esc. Save.
Tip — how to find the logical column name Go to make.powerapps.com → Tables → Book → click the Title column → scroll to Advanced options. The Logical name field shows something like cr123_title or learn_title. That exact string (with your prefix) goes inside the quotes in the Search() function. You'll need this for filter expressions in Power Automate too.
Words you learned today
  • Power Fx — the Excel-like formula language of canvas apps.
  • Gallery / Items — a scrollable list of rows / the formula that says which rows to show.
  • ThisItem — "the row displayed on this gallery card"; only valid inside a gallery template.
  • BookGallery.Selected — the row the user last clicked in the gallery; used outside the gallery.
  • Search(table, text, "column") — returns rows where the column contains the text.
  • OnSelect — the event property that runs when a button or gallery row is clicked.
  • Navigate(Screen, Transition) — navigates to another screen.
Real world — Power Fx in production apps

Power Fx is used in every Power Apps production app worldwide. Some patterns you'll see in real projects:

  • Search is almost always present: any gallery showing more than 10 rows gets a search box. The Search(table, text, "column") pattern you just learned is copy-pasted thousands of times a day by developers worldwide.
  • Filter + Search combined: real apps often combine both. For example, show only "In stock" books that match the search: Search(Filter(Books, 'In stock' = true), SearchInput.Text, "learn_title"). You'll add this in Exercise 3 below.
  • Navigation is the backbone: every multi-screen app is just Navigate() calls wired to buttons and gallery rows. Mastering navigation makes you capable of building any flow of screens a UX designer can draw.
Common mistakes with galleries and formulas
  • Using display name instead of logical name in Search(). Search(Books, text, "Title") will not work — the logical name (like "learn_title" or "cr123_title") must go in the quotes. Copy it from the column's Advanced options.
  • Editing inside the gallery template accidentally. When you double-click a gallery, you enter the template and your edits apply to that repeating row pattern. Clicking Esc exits the template. A common mistake is placing a button inside the gallery template — it then appears on every row instead of once.
  • Using ThisItem outside a gallery. ThisItem only works inside a gallery's template. Outside, use BookGallery.Selected to refer to the selected row.
Practice — Day 6 hands-on checks
  • Exercise 1: With the gallery showing all books, open the formula bar for BookGallery's Items property. Change Search(Books, SearchInput.Text, "learn_title") to just Books. Press Play — the search box no longer filters. Undo the change (Ctrl+Z) to restore Search(). This shows what the formula actually does.
  • Exercise 2: Inside the gallery template, click Body1. Change its Text formula to "Copies: " & Text(ThisItem.'Available copies') & " / " & Text(ThisItem.'Total copies'). Press Play — each card now shows the copy count like "Copies: 2 / 3". This is a real UX pattern in library apps.
  • Exercise 3: Add an in-stock filter: Insert a Toggle control above the gallery. Rename it InStockToggle. Set its Default = false. Change BookGallery's Items = Search(If(InStockToggle.Value, Filter(Books, 'In stock'), Books), SearchInput.Text, "learn_title"). Press Play — toggle ON filters to in-stock books only. This combines Filter() and Search() — the production pattern.
  • Exercise 4: Add a Label showing the count of filtered results. Set Text = Text(CountRows(BookGallery.AllItems)) & " books". Press Play and search — the count updates in real time. CountRows(Gallery.AllItems) counts the filtered rows, not all rows.
  • Exercise 5: Select BooksScreen in the tree view. Set its OnVisible property = ResetForm(BookForm). This ensures the book form clears whenever you navigate back to the books list — prevents stale data appearing if a user presses Back mid-edit.

Day 7

BookFormScreen and LoanFormScreen: forms, CRUD, and navigation

~120 minutes

Concept — the Edit Form control and its modes

A Form control is the standard way to display and edit one row from a table. It has three modes:

  • View mode — shows the row read-only. The user can see but not change data.
  • Edit mode — shows the row with editable fields. The user can change values and then save.
  • New mode — all fields are empty and blank, ready for a new row.

Key form functions: SubmitForm(FormName) — saves the row to Dataverse (creates if New mode, updates if Edit mode). NewForm(FormName) — switches to New mode. EditForm(FormName) — switches to Edit mode. ResetForm(FormName) — clears unsaved changes and returns to View mode. The form's Item property says which row it is displaying.

Do this — build BookFormScreen
  • Click BookFormScreen in the tree view. Add the same blue header rectangle (Width = App.Width, Height = 60, Fill = RGBA(47,111,237,1)) and a title Label: Text = "Book Details", Color = White, FontSize = 22, Bold.
  • Insert a Back button: Insert → Icon → Back arrow. Position it in the header at the left. Set its OnSelect = Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Back). Set Color = White.
  • Insert → Forms → Edit form. Resize it to fill most of the screen below the header. In the Tree view, rename it to BookForm.
  • With BookForm selected: in Properties pane, set Data source = Books. Click Edit fields → add all fields: Title, ISBN, Genre, Pages, Published year, Description, Available copies, Total copies, In stock, Author. Remove any fields you don't want.
  • Set BookForm's Item property = BookGallery.Selected. This links the form to whichever book was clicked in the gallery.
  • Set BookForm's DefaultMode = If(IsBlank(BookGallery.Selected), FormMode.New, FormMode.View).
    Breaking this down word by word:
    If( — start of the If function. It has three parts: condition, value-if-true, value-if-false.
    IsBlank(BookGallery.Selected) — is the gallery's selected row empty? When a user presses "+ New Book" (not clicking any existing row), there is no selected row, so this is true.
    , FormMode.New — if true (nothing selected), open the form in New mode (all blank).
    , FormMode.View — if false (a row is selected), open the form in View mode showing that row's data.
    ) — closes the If function.
    In plain English: "If nothing is selected in the gallery, open the form blank for a new entry. If something IS selected, open the form showing that book's details."
  • Set BookForm's OnSuccess = Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade). After saving, the app returns to the books list automatically.
  • Add an Edit button: Insert → Button, rename to EditBtn. Text = "Edit". OnSelect = EditForm(BookForm).
  • Add a Save button: rename to SaveBtn. Text = "Save". OnSelect = SubmitForm(BookForm). Make it visible only when editing: Visible = BookForm.Mode = FormMode.Edit || BookForm.Mode = FormMode.New.
    Breaking down the Visible formula: BookForm.Mode is the form's current mode. = FormMode.Edit checks if it's in Edit mode. || means OR. = FormMode.New checks if it's in New mode. So the full formula means: "Show this button ONLY if the form is currently in Edit mode OR New mode." When the form is in View mode (just looking at a book), the Save button hides automatically.
  • Add a Cancel button: rename to CancelBtn. Text = "Cancel". OnSelect = ResetForm(BookForm); Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade). Visible = BookForm.Mode = FormMode.Edit || BookForm.Mode = FormMode.New (same rule as Save — only visible during editing).
    Breaking down OnSelect: ResetForm(BookForm) throws away any changes the user made (cancels the edit). Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade) takes the user back to the books list. The semicolon separates the two actions — they happen in order.
  • Add a Delete button: rename to DeleteBtn. Text = "Delete". Fill = RGBA(220,53,69,1) (red colour). OnSelect = Remove(Books, BookGallery.Selected); Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade). Visible = BookForm.Mode = FormMode.View.
    Breaking down OnSelect: Remove(Books, BookGallery.Selected) — Remove deletes a row. Books is the table. BookGallery.Selected is whichever book row was clicked. Then Navigate takes the user back. Breaking down Visible: BookForm.Mode = FormMode.View — only show the Delete button when in View mode. You don't want a Delete button appearing while someone is mid-edit.
  • Add a Lend button on BookFormScreen: Text = "Lend this book". OnSelect = Navigate(LoanFormScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade). Visible = BookGallery.Selected.In stock = true (only show when book is in stock).
  • Play ▶. Click a book on BooksScreen → see it in BookFormScreen in View mode. Click Edit → fields become editable. Change something → Save → you're back at BooksScreen and the change is saved. Click New Book → form is blank → fill in → Save → new row appears. Test Delete too. Press Esc. Save the app.

Words you learned today
  • Edit form / View / Edit / New mode — the control for one row / read-only / editable / blank-new.
  • Item property — the formula that tells the form which row to display.
  • SubmitForm — saves the current form row to Dataverse (create or update).
  • NewForm / EditForm / ResetForm — switch to New / Edit / View (discarding changes) mode.
  • OnSuccess — runs automatically after a successful SubmitForm.
  • OnVisible — runs every time a screen is navigated to.
  • Remove(table, record) — deletes a row from a Dataverse table.
Real world — forms and CRUD in enterprise apps

The Edit Form pattern you built today (View → Edit → Save, with OnSuccess navigation) is the most common pattern in every data-entry app worldwide:

  • A field service company uses this exact pattern for Work Order forms: technicians view an assigned job (View mode), tap Edit to fill in completion details, tap Save, and the app navigates back to their job list.
  • An insurance company's claims app uses three form screens: ClaimsList, ClaimDetails (view/edit), and NewClaim (always New mode with DefaultMode = FormMode.New).
  • The Delete pattern with Remove() is used cautiously in production — most real apps don't delete records; they set a Status = "Inactive" instead, so data is preserved for audits. Your Library Back Office can retire books (set In stock = No) without deleting them.
Common mistakes with forms
  • Forgetting to set the Item property. If BookForm.Item is blank, the form has no data to display or edit — it appears empty in View mode. Always set Item = BookGallery.Selected (or whichever gallery feeds it).
  • Using SubmitForm in the wrong context. SubmitForm(BookForm) saves the form that was most recently in Edit or New mode. If the form is in View mode, SubmitForm does nothing. Use EditForm(BookForm) first to switch to Edit mode, then SubmitForm on the Save button.
  • Not testing OnSuccess. OnSuccess only fires after a successful save. If the form has a validation error (e.g. a Required field is blank), OnSuccess doesn't run and the navigation doesn't happen. Test by deliberately leaving a Required field blank — confirm the error appears and the user stays on the form.
  • Making Delete available in Edit/New mode. Always use Visible = BookForm.Mode = FormMode.View on the Delete button — deleting a record that was just created (New mode) or is mid-edit causes unexpected behaviour.

Mini-quiz — Day 7

Q1 What is the difference between SubmitForm(BookForm) and Patch(Books, BookGallery.Selected, {Title: "New Title"})?

Q2 You set OnSuccess = Navigate(BooksScreen, ScreenTransition.Fade) on BookForm. A user fills in a form with a blank Required field and clicks Save. Does the navigation happen?

Q3 What does DefaultMode = If(IsBlank(BookGallery.Selected), FormMode.New, FormMode.View) achieve?

Show answers

A1 SubmitForm uses the form control's field values (whatever is in the form's field inputs). Patch() directly writes specified values to a record without needing a form control. SubmitForm is for forms; Patch() is for inline data changes from buttons or logic.

A2 No. OnSuccess only fires when the save completes without errors. A Required field being blank causes a validation error — the form shows the error and OnSuccess does not run, keeping the user on the form screen.

A3 When a user presses "+ New Book", BookGallery.Selected is blank (nothing selected), so the form opens in New mode (all fields empty). When a user clicks an existing book row, BookGallery.Selected is that row, so the form opens in View mode showing that book's data.

Day 8

Library Back Office: model-driven app, views, forms, and business rules

~120 minutes

Concept — what model-driven apps give you for free

Canvas apps give you full control — but you design every button, every screen. Model-driven apps trade that control for speed and built-in features:

  • Auto-generated forms and views — the app reads your table columns and creates the form automatically. Change a column → the form updates.
  • Built-in command bars — New, Save, Delete, Refresh buttons appear automatically. No Power Fx needed.
  • Sub-grids — a related table shown inside a record (e.g. all Loans for a Member inside the Member form).
  • Activity timeline — notes, emails, tasks linked to a record.
  • Business process flows — a guided step-by-step process shown at the top of a record.
  • Always mobile-ready — the layout automatically adapts to phones and tablets.
Concept — site map, views, forms, and business rules
  • Site map — the navigation menu of a model-driven app. You define Groups (sections) and Subareas (links to tables or dashboards). Users see this on the left side of the app.
  • View — a saved, filtered, sorted list of rows for a table. Active Books, Overdue Loans, etc. Users switch between views using a dropdown.
  • Form — the layout for a single record. You arrange fields into sections and tabs. A record can have multiple forms (one for quick entry, one for detailed view).
  • Business rule — "if this, then that" logic attached to a table, written in a visual designer with no code. Runs in model-driven apps, canvas apps, and even the Dataverse data grid — everywhere the data is edited.

Real world — model-driven apps and business rules in enterprise

Model-driven apps power some of the world's largest CRM and ERP systems. Dynamics 365 — used by thousands of Fortune 500 companies — is entirely model-driven Power Platform. When you built Library Back Office today, you built something architecturally identical to a Dynamics 365 module:

  • The site map you configured is the same left-navigation structure Dynamics 365 users see every day.
  • Business rules in enterprise apps automate compliance: an insurance company's business rule says "if ClaimAmount > 50000, set Approval required = Yes" — exactly the same as your Loan Overdue rule, but for a financial process.
  • Sub-grids are ubiquitous in enterprise forms: every customer record in a CRM shows a sub-grid of their purchases, their support tickets, and their contacts — the same pattern as your Loan History sub-grid in the Member form.

You are not just learning Power Platform — you are learning how Dynamics 365, and enterprise data platforms in general, are built.

Common mistakes with model-driven apps and business rules
  • Not publishing after changes. Changes to views, forms, or business rules in the App Designer don't take effect until you click Publish. A common mistake is testing before publishing and wondering why nothing changed.
  • Business rules don't run in flows. A business rule runs when a record is opened or saved in an app — it does NOT run when a Power Automate flow updates a record via the API. For flow-triggered data changes, you need flow logic (conditions + update row actions).
  • Forgetting to Activate a business rule. After saving a business rule, its status is Draft. It won't run until you click Activate. Check the status icon on the business rules list.
  • Adding too many fields to views. A view with 15 columns becomes unreadable on screen. Stick to 5-7 of the most important columns. More detail belongs in the form (which you see when you click a row).
