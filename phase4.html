What "AI" Really Means — Before We Touch Any Tool

AI is not magic — it is mathematics

AI is statistics — very sophisticated statistics. When a chatbot reads your question and gives an answer, it is doing this:

  1. Your question is broken into numbers — this is called tokenization
  2. A very large mathematical model trained on billions of sentences finds the most likely next word, then the next, then the next
  3. Those words are assembled into a response

The AI does not "know" anything in the way a human knows things. It has seen patterns in text and learned which words tend to follow which other words, in which context. It is extremely good at this — good enough that the output seems like understanding.

What AI does well vs what AI does badly
AI does wellAI does badly
Generating natural-sounding textKnowing current facts (only knows its training data)
Summarising long documentsDoing precise arithmetic reliably
Translating between languagesFollowing strict business rules without error
Classifying inputs into categoriesKnowing when it is wrong (can be confidently incorrect)
Extracting information from unstructured textRespecting data privacy if not carefully configured
Predicting patterns in dataMaking decisions that require human judgment or empathy
💭 The most important question to ask about any AI feature

"What happens when the AI is wrong?"

Every AI feature needs a fallback — a way for humans to catch and correct AI errors before they affect real people. AI should make your system better, not make your system fragile. Design every AI feature with a human-in-the-loop for low-confidence or high-stakes decisions.

Vocabulary for Week 13
1. Machine Learning
A category of AI where a computer learns patterns from examples instead of being given explicit rules.
Analogy: Teaching a child to recognise cats. You show thousands of pictures and say "cat" each time. After many examples, the child recognises cats in new pictures they have never seen — without ever receiving a list of rules.
Example: "The AI Builder model learned to recognise overdue notices by looking at 200 examples of overdue notices and 200 examples of other documents. Now it can classify new documents with 94% accuracy."
2. Model (in AI)
The result of machine learning — the mathematical structure that learned from examples and can now make predictions on new data.
Analogy: A doctor who has treated 10,000 patients. All those cases are their "training data." Their medical judgment is the "model" — a pattern-recognition system built from experience.
Example: "I trained an AI Builder model to classify library book return condition: Good, Damaged, or Lost. The model is a file that stores what it learned. I deploy this model to production and it classifies 100 book returns per day automatically."
3. Training (in AI)
The process of teaching an AI model by showing it many labeled examples.
Example: "Training the membership form reader took 45 minutes: I uploaded 50 completed membership forms, highlighted the Name, Email, and Phone fields on each form, and clicked Train. The model learned where these fields are on the form."
4. Prediction
The AI model's output — what it thinks the answer is for a new input.
Example: "I sent a new membership form to the trained model. The model's prediction: Name = 'Aditya Kumar', Email = 'aditya.kumar@email.com', Phone = '+91-9876501234', Confidence = 97%. The 97% confidence means the model is highly certain about this prediction."
5. Confidence Score
A percentage that tells you how certain the AI is about its prediction — 99% means very sure, 40% means unsure.
Critical rule: Always set a minimum confidence threshold below which you ask a human to verify. Never automatically trust a prediction below 70%.
Example: "The form reader returned a phone number with 42% confidence. The flow is programmed: if confidence < 70%, send the form to Pooja for manual review. If confidence ≥ 70%, save the extracted data automatically."
6. Azure AI Language
An Azure service that understands text — it can detect language, analyse sentiment, extract key information, and answer questions from documents.
Example: "I used Azure AI Language to analyse member complaint emails. The service returns: sentiment = 'negative', key phrases = ['damaged book', 'spine broken', 'replacement request']. The flow automatically routes high-priority negative sentiment emails to Pooja."
7. Sentiment Analysis
AI-powered analysis of whether text is positive, negative, or neutral.
Analogy: A mood ring — but for text. It does not always get it right, but it gives you a quick indicator.
Example: "I ran sentiment analysis on 6 months of member feedback forms. Result: 72% positive (members happy), 18% neutral, 10% negative. The negative feedback clusters around two themes: parking (outside our control) and late fee amounts (actionable)."
8. Named Entity Recognition (NER)
AI that identifies specific types of information in text — names, dates, amounts, locations, organisations.
Example: "I used NER on member emails to automatically extract: who they are mentioning (person names), which books they are discussing (titles), and what dates they reference. The flow uses these extracted entities to link the email to the correct Dataverse records."

💭 System Thinking — What happens when AI Builder credits run out?

AI Builder models cost "AI credits" from your Microsoft license. Each prediction uses credits. If credits run out mid-month, the AI features stop working. If your overdue email classification flow depends on AI Builder and credits are exhausted, emails stop being classified.

Good system design for AI:

  1. Monitor credit consumption monthly
  2. Have a fallback: if AI is unavailable, process manually (or flag all emails for human review)
  3. AI should be an enhancement, not a single point of failure

The principle: AI should make your system better, not make your system fragile.

Exercise 1 — Train a Document Processing Model for Membership Forms

This model will read membership application forms and automatically extract the member's name, email, phone, and membership type.

Step 1: Create sample membership forms

Create 5–10 PDF or image files that look like membership application forms. Each should have: Full Name, Email, Phone, Membership Type (checkbox), Date. You can create them in Word and save as PDF, or scan handwritten forms.

Step 2: Train the model

  • Go to make.powerapps.com → left menu → AI hub (or AI Builder)
  • Click + New model → Document processing
  • Name: Membership Form Reader → upload your 5–10 sample forms
  • On each form, draw a box around each field and label it: "FullName", "Email", "Phone", "MembershipType", "Date"
  • After all forms are labeled → click Train → wait 15–30 minutes for training to complete

Step 3: Test the model

  • After training, click Quick test
  • Upload a new form — one the model has NOT seen during training
  • See the extracted fields and confidence scores. Note any fields with low confidence (< 70%).

Step 4: Use the model in a Power Automate flow

  • Create a new flow: When a new email arrives (with membership form attachment)
  • Add step: Predict (AI Builder) → select your Membership Form Reader model → Input: the email attachment
  • Add step: Create a row in Dataverse → Members table → use extracted fields (FullName, Email, Phone, MembershipType)
  • Add condition: if confidence < 70% → send form to Pooja for manual review. If ≥ 70% → auto-save to Dataverse.
  • Test the flow by emailing a sample form — verify the member record appears in Dataverse.
💭 What you just built A new member scans and emails their membership form. Power Automate receives the email, AI Builder reads the form, extracts the member's information, and creates the member record in Dataverse. If the AI is not confident enough, a human reviews it. This is AI augmenting a human workflow — not replacing humans. The human is still in the loop for low-confidence cases. This is responsible AI design.

  • Create a flow: When a new row is added to a "MemberFeedback" Dataverse table

💭 System Thinking — What should the library do with the "high risk" prediction?

If the model says a member is high risk, the library has choices:

  • Block them from borrowing (punitive — dangerous and unfair)
  • Send proactive reminders earlier (preventive — recommended)
  • Offer a shorter loan period (adaptive — reasonable)
  • Call them personally when a book is due (personalized — resource-intensive)

All four are valid — they reflect different library philosophies. The AI gives you the insight. YOU (and the library team) decide what to do with it. Never automate the policy decision itself based purely on AI output, especially when it affects people's access to services.

🗣️ English Practice — Week 13: Hedging Language — Speaking Carefully About Uncertainty

Why This Grammar Pattern?

AI is powerful — but it is NOT certain. One of the most important professional skills when working with AI is knowing how to communicate about things that are PROBABLE but not GUARANTEED. This is called hedging language.

Without hedging (overconfident — dangerous):

  • "The AI model correctly identifies all overdue members."
  • "The sentiment analysis always detects negative feedback."

With hedging (accurate — professional):

  • "The AI model correctly identifies most overdue members, with an accuracy of approximately 94% on our test data."
  • "The sentiment analysis tends to detect negative feedback effectively, though it may miss sarcasm or culturally specific expressions."

Key Hedging Words and Phrases

CategoryWordsExample
Frequencyusually, generally, often, sometimes"The model generally returns results within 200ms."
Certaintylikely, probably, possibly, may, might, could"Low confidence scores may indicate a poor quality form scan."
Approximationapproximately, around, about, roughly"Training takes approximately 30 minutes."
Limitationin most cases, under normal conditions"The form reader works well in most cases, but may struggle with handwritten text."
Sourceaccording to, based on, our data suggests"Based on our test dataset, the model achieved 94% accuracy."

🚫 Common English Mistakes — Week 13

Mistake 1: "The AI thinks / believes / knows"
AI does not think, believe, or know.
❌ "The AI thinks this member will go overdue."
✅ "The model predicts that this member has a higher risk of going overdue."

Mistake 2: Reporting only the success rate
❌ "Our model is 94% accurate."
✅ "Our model is approximately 94% accurate on our test dataset of 200 loan records. This accuracy may vary on new data, particularly if member behaviour or library policies change."

Mistake 3: "AI is intelligent"
❌ "The AI is intelligent enough to understand member behaviour."
✅ "The model identifies statistical patterns in historical member behaviour."

Mistake 4: Confusing "accuracy" and "precision" (in AI context)
Accuracy = of ALL predictions, what fraction were correct.
Precision = of predictions that said "overdue", what fraction were actually overdue.
Always specify which metric you are reporting in your AI Model Card.

Vocabulary for Week 14
9. Generative AI
AI that creates new content — text, images, code — rather than just classifying or analysing existing content.
Analogy: The difference between a librarian who looks up answers in a reference book (traditional AI) and a librarian who explains concepts in their own words (generative AI).
Example: "Copilot Studio's generative answers feature uses generative AI — it does not just retrieve a stored answer. It reads our borrowing policy and writes a fresh, conversational answer for each question."
10. Conversational Design
The practice of designing how a conversation between a user and a bot flows — what the bot says, when it asks for clarification, how it handles misunderstandings.
Analogy: Writing a script for a telephone helpline. If the caller says X, the agent responds Y. If the caller seems confused, the agent says Z. The script is conversational design.
Example: "Good conversational design means the bot does not ask 5 questions at once. It asks one, waits for the answer, then asks the next. This feels like talking to a person, not filling out a form."
11. Escalation
When the chatbot cannot handle a request and transfers the user to a human agent.
Critical design principle: Every chatbot MUST have a clear escalation path. Users get frustrated when the bot loops endlessly and never offers a human.
Example: "If a member asks 'I lost a book and cannot afford to pay the replacement fee,' the bot escalates to Pooja — this requires human judgment, empathy, and possibly a payment plan discussion that no bot should handle."
12. Slot Filling
When the bot needs multiple pieces of information and collects them one by one in a natural conversation.
Example: "To reserve a book, the bot needs: book title, member ID, and preferred pickup date. Slot filling means the bot asks for each piece of information it is missing, in a natural way, until it has everything needed."
Concept — From Question-Answering Bot to Action-Taking Agent

In September, we built a Library Helper bot that answered questions from knowledge documents. That was impressive — but limited. In December, we build an agent — a bot that not only answers questions but also DOES THINGS:

September Bot (Q&A only)December Agent (Action-Taking)
"What is the borrowing limit?" → reads policy"What is my current loan status?" → queries live Dataverse
"What are library hours?" → reads FAQ"Renew my loan for Atomic Habits" → updates Dataverse record
"How do I report a damaged book?" → gives instructions"I want to report a damaged book" → creates Dataverse record AND emails Pooja
"What books do you have on AI?" → static answer"What books do you have on AI?" → searches live book catalog

The difference is live data access and write operations. The September bot read documents. The December agent reads live databases and creates records. This is the direction AI is moving: from chatbots that answer to agents that act.

💭 Security Risk — Why asking for email is not the best approach

If the user types their email, Rohit could type Priyanka's email and see her loan status. Nothing verifies the user owns that email.

Better approach: Use Teams identity. The bot's authentication session knows who is logged in. Use @{triggeredBy/userPrincipalName} — the logged-in user's email, automatically. This cannot be spoofed. This is called authenticated access — every serious chatbot uses it.

Enhancement 2: "Renew My Loan" — Write Operation

Create a new Topic called Renew Loan with trigger phrases: renew my book, extend my loan, I need more time, renew, extend

  • Bot shows the user's current active loans and asks: "Which book would you like to renew?"
  • Bot calls a Power Automate flow to check: is renewal allowed? (Max 1 renewal per book, only if not overdue)
  • If allowed: flow updates the Loan's DueDate = current DueDate + 14 days → Bot says: "Done! [Book] is now due on [new date]."
  • If not allowed: Bot says: "I'm sorry, this loan cannot be renewed — it has already been renewed once, or it is overdue. Please return the book or contact a librarian."
💭 System Thinking — Where should the "max 1 renewal" rule live?

If the rule is in the bot's Topics, it only applies when users talk to the bot. If a librarian renews a loan directly in the model-driven app, or a Power Automate flow automatically renews, the rule is bypassed.

The renewal rule must be a Dataverse Business Rule or enforced in the Power Automate flow — so that ALL paths respect it. The bot is just one entry point. Business rules belong to the data layer, not the conversation layer.

Enhancement 3: "Report a Problem" — Slot Filling + Dataverse Record + Escalation

💭 Why doesn't Microsoft always use the most powerful model?

Model choice is always a trade-off:

  • Cost: GPT-4 costs significantly more per query. 1,000 questions/day × ₹0.05 = ₹18,250/year vs ₹0.005 = ₹1,825/year
  • Latency: GPT-4 may take 2–4 seconds. Smaller models: 0.5 seconds. Users notice delays.
  • Privacy: Some organisations cannot send data to external servers. Azure OpenAI keeps data within your chosen region.

The "best" model is the one that meets your specific requirements at acceptable cost — not always the most powerful one.

PartConnectors

Concept — AI Ethics: The Three Problems Every System Builder Must Understand

The Fairness Problem — Algorithmic Bias

Suppose the prediction model finds that members from a certain area of the city tend to return books late more often. The AI will use "postal code" as a predictive feature.

But what if certain neighbourhoods have lower income? The AI learned from historical data that reflects real-world inequalities — and now it perpetuates those inequalities through automated decisions. This is algorithmic bias.

What to do: Remove features that are proxies for protected characteristics (income, location, community). Test the model's output for disparate impact. Regularly audit AI decisions that affect people's access to services.

The Accountability Problem

When the AI recommends blocking a member and Pooja follows the recommendation — who is accountable if the recommendation was wrong? The AI does not face consequences. Pooja does. Microsoft does not face consequences. Your organisation does.

Rule: Every AI-driven decision that affects a person must have a human accountable for it. AI can recommend. Humans must decide.

The Transparency Problem

If Rohit is blocked from borrowing books because the AI flagged him as "high risk," does he have the right to know why? Does he have the right to appeal? In many countries, yes — there are legal requirements (GDPR in Europe, similar laws being developed in India).

Good practice: If an AI decision affects someone, be able to explain what factors led to that decision, in plain language that the affected person can understand.

🗣️ English Practice — Week 15: Balanced Arguments

The 5-Part Structure

  1. Introduce the topic: "The question of whether [X] is not straightforward."
  2. First side: "On one hand, [argument for]. This is supported by [evidence/reason]."
  3. Second side: "On the other hand, [argument against]. Proponents argue that [reason]."
  4. Nuance: "It is also worth noting that [complicating factor]."
  5. Conclusion: "On balance, [your conclusion], because [primary reason]. However, [acknowledged limitation]."

🔊 Read Aloud Exercise — Full Balanced Argument

Read this out loud slowly. The sentences are longer — pause at every full stop:

"The question of whether to use AI predictions to identify library members at risk of going overdue is not straightforward. On one hand, early identification allows the library to send proactive reminders to members who are most likely to need them, potentially reducing overdue rates and the administrative burden of chasing unpaid fines. This benefit is real and measurable. On the other hand, the prediction model was trained on historical data that may reflect existing inequalities — for example, if members from certain postcodes or income groups have historically had higher overdue rates due to circumstances beyond their control, the model may unfairly target these groups for additional scrutiny. It is also worth noting that even a highly accurate model will make mistakes — some members flagged as high risk will always return books on time, and some low-risk members will go overdue. Treating people differently based on a probabilistic prediction, rather than their actual behaviour, raises concerns about fairness and dignity. On balance, using AI predictions to send earlier reminders — rather than to restrict access — appears to be a proportionate use of this technology, provided that all members are treated with equal courtesy and that the model's outputs are reviewed regularly for evidence of disproportionate impact on any group."

After reading: write the 5 parts of the balanced argument structure in your journal, with one sentence from the paragraph for each part.

🚫 Common English Mistakes — Week 15

Mistake 1: Weak conclusions with "It depends"
❌ "In conclusion, it depends on the situation."
✅ "On balance, [specific recommendation], because [specific reason], provided that [specific safeguard]."

Mistake 2: "Bias" used vaguely
❌ "The AI model has bias."
✅ "The AI model shows demographic bias — specifically, it disproportionately flags members from [specific group] as high-risk, at a rate 1.4× higher than other groups with similar actual overdue frequencies."
Always specify: which group, what direction, how large the effect.

Mistake 3: "Moreover" used as "Also"
"Moreover" and "Furthermore" mean "and this is an ADDITIONAL, STRONGER point."
❌ "AI can predict overdue loans. Moreover, it can also analyse sentiment."
✅ "AI can predict overdue loans. Moreover, these predictions have proven more accurate than staff intuitions based on manual review." (this IS a stronger claim)
